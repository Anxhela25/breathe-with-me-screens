Breathe With Me: Technology and Decisions

Breathe With Me is an iPhone app that guides a person through a panic attack with a breathing circle, a 5-4-3-2-1 grounding exercise and a short AI chat that is safety-checked at every step. A Python backend keeps accounts, history and the safety logic. This page lists the technologies we used and why, then the main architecture and design decisions with the evidence behind them. Numbers in square brackets point to the references at the end.

Architecture decisions

The main rule behind the architecture: help must work instantly and safely, even when the internet, the AI or the account does not.

DecisionWhat we builtWhy
Help works without an accountBreathing, grounding, the chat and urgent help need no login. History is saved only with an account.In a panic, nobody should be stopped by a sign-up form. Health data is stored only with explicit consent, as GDPR requires for health data [26].
Safety never depends on the AIEvery chat message passes four checks: fixed rules for crisis words first, then an AI risk check, then the AI reply, then a check of that reply.Leading AI chatbots answer suicide-related questions inconsistently [13]. Clear crisis messages never reach the AI.
Fail safe, not silentIf the risk check gets no answer, the chat asks "Are you safe?". If no AI answers within 10 seconds, a fixed calm message shows.An outage must lead to a safer step, never to no reply or an unchecked one.
Crisis words match typos"i wnat to diee" or "kill myselv" count as crisis words.People in distress type fast. A false alarm is better than a missed crisis.
Chat in English only, 6 messagesOther languages get the breathing and grounding options. After 6 messages the chat points to people.The safety word lists are in English only, so other languages can't be checked. A long chat is not a replacement for a person.
Crisis pause on phone and serverAfter a crisis message the pause is saved on the phone and, with an account, on the server.It holds after closing the app, without internet and on every device.
Two login keysA 1-hour access key and a 90-day refresh key that is replaced on every use, kept in the iPhone Keychain [23, 24].People open the app when an attack comes, often weeks apart. They should never be logged out at that moment, and a copied key stops working.
Passwords and codesAt least 12 characters; common and leaked passwords refused [21, 25]. Email codes last 15 minutes, work once, are stored as a hash and allow 5 tries [22].Length protects better than forced symbols [21]. Only part of a hash leaves the server for the leak check [25].
Calm check on the phoneOnly the average colour of each camera frame is used. The pulse is found by signal processing, not AI.The video never leaves the phone.
Private by defaultChat messages are never saved. Logs and error reports never contain emails, passwords or chat text. The doctor PDF is made on the phone.It's health data. Only what the user chooses to share leaves the device.
Database changes as migrationsRender runs the migrations before starting the API on every deploy.The database always matches the code, locally and online.

Design decisions

Each value below is either taken from research, taken from a published standard, or set by us with a reason. We never present a design choice as a medical fact.

DecisionWhyBasis
4 s in, 6 s out
6 breaths a minute		6 breaths a minute is the rate most used in heart-rate-variability research, where breathing and heart rhythm move together [3, 4]. A longer out-breath improved mood in a randomised trial [6]. No exact rhythm has been shown to be better than another [5], so we chose the standard one, which the calm check also uses. The circle asks for slow breathing, never deep breathing [27].Research
2 minutes before "How are you now?"Research protocols hold each breathing rate for 2 minutes [7], and most of the short-term heart-rate change appears within the first 2 minutes [8].Research protocol
Four word answersI'm fine now, Better, About the same, Worse. Anxiety takes up attention and working memory [9]. Picking one of four words needs less thought than rating, and rating scales are not precise measures anyway [10].Design choice, informed by [9, 10]
"About the same" twice leads to peopleTwo blocks of help without change show the attack is lasting. That is when calling someone or emergency services is suggested, without alarming the user too early.Design choice
"Worse" first checks for a medical emergencyChest pain, trouble breathing, pain in the arm or jaw and fainting can also be heart problems, so these are ruled out before more breathing.Design choice, for safety
20-minute noteA panic attack peaks within minutes [1] and most last 5 to 20 minutes [2]. After 20 minutes the app suggests contacting a doctor.Clinical guidance
24-hour chat pause after a crisisIn one study, almost half of suicide attempts came 10 minutes or less after the first suicidal thought, and many people were still open to contact with family or friends [14]. Care guidance recommends a follow-up call within 24 hours after leaving care [15]. AI is not reliable at this point [13]. For 24 hours the chat shows people to call instead of the AI, and there is no button to end it early.Design choice, informed by [13, 14, 15]
Grounding order: see, touch, hear, smell, tasteGrounding moves attention to the present surroundings [11]. Sight is the dominant sense [12], so the exercise starts with the most items and the easiest sense, and ends with the senses that are hardest to use. It's also the standard order people may already know.Standard practice [11, 12]
Calm check in words, never numbers for the rhythmPhone camera heart rate agrees with ECG under good conditions but varies with the phone [19]. One minute is enough for a basic resting measure [20]. So the rhythm result is shown as kind words, compared only with the user's own earlier checks.Research [19, 20]

Colours and touch targets

The palette is soft and muted: deep sage #4A6355 for the main buttons and text, dark green #2F4136 for headings, pistachio #E2EADA for the breathing circle and warm cream #F7F6F1 for the background. Emergency buttons use a soft pink #F6E1DC with deep rose text #8E433B, so they stand out without being scary. Evidence that specific colours reduce anxiety is limited [16], so the palette is a design choice, not a treatment. What we can measure is readability:

Text on backgroundContrastWCAG 2.2 AA, normal text (4.5:1) [17]
Dark green on cream10.1 : 1Pass
White on the sage button6.5 : 1Pass
Sage on cream6.1 : 1Pass
Rose on the pink emergency button5.5 : 1Pass
Sage on the pistachio circle5.3 : 1Pass

The main buttons are 48 to 60 points tall, above Apple's 44-point minimum [18], so they are easy to hit with shaky hands.
